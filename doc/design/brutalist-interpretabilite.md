# Presentation Style Guide

## Swiss Brutalism × Editorial Paper — Landscape Slides

---

# 1. Overall intention

Create a presentation system inspired by **contemporary Swiss Brutalism**, combined with a very light editorial paper materiality.

The slides should feel closer to:

* research posters;
* scientific editorial spreads;
* contemporary lecture decks;
* independent publishing layouts;
* visual essays;
* institutional presentations with a strong graphic identity.

The objective is not to decorate information, but to give it a clear visual hierarchy and a recognizable rhythm.

Every slide must belong to the same visual family while retaining enough freedom to avoid repetition.

---

# 2. Core principles

The presentation system is built around six principles:

1. strong typographic hierarchy;
2. limited visual density;
3. active use of white space;
4. asymmetrical but controlled composition;
5. a dominant blue identity color;
6. restrained use of four secondary colors as visual counterpoints.

The result should feel:

* direct;
* editorial;
* intellectual;
* contemporary;
* structured;
* slightly brutalist;
* never decorative or futuristic.

---

# 3. Slide format

## Default format

* Landscape presentation
* Aspect ratio: **16:9**
* Recommended size: **1920 × 1080 px**
* White background: `#FFFFFF`
* Full-bleed composition
* No permanent frame
* No visible outer border

## Safe area

Keep a generous internal safe area:

* left and right margins: 6–8% of slide width;
* top and bottom margins: 6–9% of slide height.

These margins may be intentionally broken by one dominant element.

---

# 4. Color system

The presentation uses a deliberately restricted palette.

## Main text color

* Matte black: `#1A1A1A`

## Dominant identity color

* Study Blue: `#0047AB`

Blue is the primary visual code of the deck.

It represents:

* the main conceptual thread;
* a key variable;
* a revealed mechanism;
* an internal representation;
* an important scientific result;
* the current section or narrative focus.

Blue should remain visually dominant across the full presentation.

---

# 5. The four-color counterpoint system

The secondary palette is composed of four bright colors:

* Yellow: `#FFD600`
* Red: `#E63946`
* Orange: `#F77F00`
* Green: `#06A77D`

These colors are allowed together as a **micro-accent quartet**, not as four competing dominant colors.

## Core rule

The four colors may appear simultaneously only when they behave like editorial registration marks, analytical signals, or small categorical indicators.

They must never carry equal visual weight to the blue.

## Recommended ratio

Across a typical slide:

* Blue: 70–85% of all colored surface;
* Black: main text and structure;
* Four-color quartet combined: 5–15% of all colored surface.

## Practical device: the “Four-Point Counterpoint”

Use the four secondary colors as four small recurring marks placed at the edge of the composition.

Possible forms:

* four short bars;
* four small squares;
* four circular markers;
* four underlines;
* four tiny page tabs;
* four endpoint dots on a line;
* four category ticks in a chart.

The marks should be small, flat, and consistent throughout the deck.

They function like printing registration marks or editorial indexing devices.

## Additional rule

On most slides, use only one of the four secondary colors as the active counterpoint.

The remaining three may appear only as tiny persistent markers, section tabs, or category references.

This creates a system in which:

* blue provides continuity;
* one secondary color gives local emphasis;
* the full quartet preserves series identity.

## Forbidden use

Do not use all four colors as:

* large background blocks;
* equally sized quadrants;
* rainbow gradients;
* decorative confetti;
* multicolored headlines;
* competing chart palettes without semantic purpose.

---

# 6. Typography

Use one sans-serif family throughout the deck.

Recommended families:

* Helvetica Neue;
* Inter;
* IBM Plex Sans;
* Archivo.

Allowed weights:

* Black;
* Bold;
* Regular.

Avoid:

* serif typefaces;
* decorative fonts;
* script fonts;
* multiple font families;
* excessive use of italics;
* outlined type;
* 3D text;
* typographic shadows;
* text gradients.

---

# 7. Typographic scale

The scale should be highly contrasted.

## Suggested hierarchy

### Hero word or title

* 80–180 pt equivalent
* Black or Bold
* May occupy 30–60% of the slide
* May extend beyond the slide edge

### Section title

* 44–72 pt
* Black or Bold

### Main statement

* 30–48 pt
* Bold or Regular

### Body text

* 22–30 pt
* Regular

### Caption or source

* 14–20 pt
* Regular

Use fewer words rather than smaller type.

---

# 8. Slide composition

The composition is asymmetrical and editorial.

It does not rely on a visible grid, but on controlled tension between:

* text blocks;
* images;
* geometric forms;
* colored accents;
* large empty areas.

Principles:

* irregular but intentional margins;
* large fields of white space;
* no systematic centering;
* no repetitive two-column template on every slide;
* limited number of alignments;
* one dominant anchor per slide;
* one possible rotation per slide;
* one possible bleed beyond the frame;
* captions placed where the composition naturally allows them.

The empty area is part of the design.

---

# 9. Horizontal rhythm

Landscape slides naturally encourage left-to-right reading.

Use this direction carefully.

Possible structures:

* dominant word on the left, evidence on the right;
* image on the far right, annotation crossing toward the center;
* large horizontal statement with a small interruption;
* timeline moving from left to right;
* comparison built around two unequal visual masses;
* one object centered off-axis with text anchored to one edge.

Avoid treating every slide as a standard split-screen layout.

---

# 10. Signature elements

Each slide should include at least three of the following:

* one dominant word, number, or statement;
* one blue structural element;
* one small secondary-color counterpoint;
* one off-grid alignment;
* one controlled bleed;
* one thin editorial line;
* one small floating caption;
* one large area of white space.

Across the full deck, the recurring signature should include:

* dominant blue;
* black typography;
* the four-color micro-accent system;
* asymmetrical layouts;
* sparse paper materiality;
* strong scale contrast.

---

# 11. Slide families

To create rhythm, alternate between several slide families.

## A. Opening slide

Purpose:

* establish the main theme;
* create immediate visual identity.

Characteristics:

* one very large title or keyword;
* one small subtitle;
* one blue element;
* one four-color micro-accent marker;
* generous white space.

## B. Section divider

Purpose:

* mark a new chapter.

Characteristics:

* section number or keyword at very large scale;
* limited text;
* blue dominant area or blue typographic mass;
* one active secondary color.

## C. Core idea slide

Purpose:

* express one central proposition.

Characteristics:

* one sentence;
* one diagram, image, or visual object;
* minimal supporting text;
* no more than three informational levels.

## D. Evidence slide

Purpose:

* present data, a quote, a study, or a result.

Characteristics:

* one figure or one data point dominates;
* source remains visible but secondary;
* blue highlights the key result;
* secondary colors may encode categories.

## E. Comparison slide

Purpose:

* show a contrast, tension, or before/after relationship.

Characteristics:

* two unequal zones rather than two symmetrical boxes;
* one side may use black, the other blue;
* one secondary color identifies the key difference.

## F. Process slide

Purpose:

* explain a mechanism or sequence.

Characteristics:

* three to five steps maximum;
* horizontal progression;
* minimal arrows;
* use color only to clarify transitions.

## G. Closing slide

Purpose:

* leave one strong conclusion.

Characteristics:

* one final statement;
* very limited supporting text;
* return to the opening visual motif;
* use the four-color counterpoint as a closing signature.

---

# 12. Text density

Each slide should communicate one main idea.

Recommended limits:

* one title;
* one main statement;
* one short explanation;
* one source or caption;
* three to five bullets only when necessary.

Avoid:

* long paragraphs;
* dense bullet lists;
* miniaturized text;
* report pages copied directly into slides;
* dashboards overloaded with labels;
* more than three visual messages on one slide.

When content is complex, split it into several slides.

---

# 13. Geometric forms

Use simple forms only:

* rectangles;
* bars;
* lines;
* circles;
* flat blocks;
* thin frames;
* cropped planes;
* index marks.

These forms must serve a function:

* organize;
* connect;
* separate;
* label;
* emphasize;
* indicate progression;
* encode categories.

They must not be used as decorative filler.

---

# 14. Illustrations and photography

Figurative images are allowed when they directly represent the subject.

Accepted subjects include:

* people;
* classrooms;
* animals;
* plants;
* scientific instruments;
* laboratories;
* documents;
* computers;
* objects;
* landscapes;
* physical experiments.

## Recommended treatment

* semi-realistic paper-cut silhouette;
* clean cropping;
* reduced details;
* isolated subject;
* flat or nearly flat rendering;
* minimal shadow;
* discreet integration into the composition.

The image must support the content rather than dominate the slide.

## Photography

Photography may be used when:

* it provides documentary value;
* it shows a real place, object, experiment, or person;
* it is cropped strongly;
* it is integrated as a single visual mass.

Avoid decorative stock photography.

---

# 15. Materiality

The slides remain mostly flat.

A very subtle paper quality may be introduced through:

* fine paper grain;
* slight white-on-white layering;
* micro-relief;
* soft overlapping paper planes;
* almost invisible surface variation.

The paper effect must remain secondary.

Avoid:

* thick cardboard;
* torn paper;
* visible glue or tape;
* scrapbook effects;
* heavy fiber texture;
* handmade collage aesthetics;
* exaggerated depth.

---

# 16. Shadows

Shadows should only suggest slight overlap.

## Light direction

* upper left

## Recommended settings

* blur: 20–30 px;
* opacity: 8–12%;
* minimal offset;
* neutral tone.

Never use:

* hard shadows;
* black drop shadows;
* dramatic depth;
* floating-card effects;
* 3D extrusion;
* glossy highlights.

---

# 17. Charts and data visualizations

Charts should remain editorial and sparse.

Principles:

* remove unnecessary gridlines;
* highlight one key value;
* use blue for the main series;
* use one secondary color for the comparison series;
* reserve the remaining colors for small categorical markers;
* label data directly whenever possible;
* avoid legends when direct annotation is clearer.

The four-color quartet may be used together only when the data genuinely contains four distinct categories.

Even then, blue remains the framing or reference color.

---

# 18. Diagrams

Diagrams should be reduced to the essential mechanism.

Use:

* few nodes;
* short labels;
* thin lines;
* minimal arrows;
* direct annotations;
* clear causal direction.

Avoid:

* complex network maps;
* generic technology icons;
* excessive connectors;
* decorative circuits;
* pseudo-scientific visual complexity.

---

# 19. Transitions and animation

Animations should be restrained.

Preferred transitions:

* appear;
* fade;
* simple wipe;
* progressive reveal;
* object movement along a straight path.

Use animation only to:

* reveal a sequence;
* isolate a key point;
* compare two states;
* explain a mechanism.

Avoid:

* spinning;
* bouncing;
* dramatic zooms;
* 3D transitions;
* decorative motion;
* multiple simultaneous effects.

---

# 20. Visual metaphors to avoid

Do not represent artificial intelligence, cognition, or data through generic technological clichés.

Avoid:

* digital brains;
* connected brains;
* humanoid robots;
* robotic faces;
* anthropomorphic chips;
* glowing networks;
* decorative neural networks;
* circuit-board patterns;
* holograms;
* futuristic interfaces;
* digital eyes;
* gears;
* lightbulbs;
* black boxes;
* giant lenses;
* padlocks;
* abstract data clouds;
* data vortices;
* glowing streams;
* human silhouettes filled with circuitry.

Represent concepts through:

* their real object;
* observable effects;
* scientific evidence;
* experimental setup;
* documented result;
* actual situation;
* clear diagram.

---

# 21. Recommended presentation rhythm

A strong deck should alternate intensity.

Example rhythm:

1. bold opening slide;
2. quiet contextual slide;
3. large statement slide;
4. evidence slide;
5. visual or diagram slide;
6. comparison slide;
7. section divider;
8. dense but controlled analytical slide;
9. quiet synthesis slide;
10. strong closing slide.

Avoid using the same composition more than twice in succession.

---

# 22. Final visual objective

The final presentation should evoke:

* a scientific editorial publication;
* a contemporary research conference deck;
* an independent design journal;
* a typographic poster series;
* a precise visual argument.

It should be:

* clear;
* sparse;
* memorable;
* coherent;
* intellectually credible;
* visually distinctive;
* restrained in its use of color;
* free from decorative technological clichés.

The dominant blue ensures continuity.

The four secondary colors act as a light editorial counterpoint: visible enough to create rhythm, but never strong enough to weaken the blue identity of the presentation.
